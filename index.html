Why this briefing matters now

HeyGen video placeholder

Governors and SLT are ultimately accountable for how your school meets its SEND duties, but the accountability landscape underneath that has shifted substantially over the last year, across funding conditions, emerging duties, and inspection. If this is not briefed clearly, whoever holds accountability ends up finding out about gaps from an inspection report rather than in good time.

Governors typically need the strategic picture: what they are accountable for and what to ask. SLT also needs the operational detail underneath it, since SLT are the ones translating governor-level decisions into staff deployment, training schedules and day-to-day practice. The same three-question structure works for both, but expect SLT conversations to spend longer on the "what do we need from you" section, since the answer usually involves timetabling, staffing and budget decisions governors will not be making directly.

Ofsted's current inspection framework

Image placeholderHyper-realistic image reflecting inspection and accountability · 16:9

Unlike the Inclusion Strategy and ISPs, this part is not a future proposal. Ofsted's reformed inspection framework has been in effect since 10 November 2025, and it is what any inspection from that date onwards will use.

Old grading vs new report card

Before:

a single overall word, such as "Good" or "Outstanding," covering the whole school.

Now:

a report card with six graded evaluation areas, each scored on a five-point scale from Urgent Improvement up to Exceptional, plus safeguarding graded separately as either "met" or "not met."

Grading now uses a "secure fit" model. A school must meet every standard within a graded area to be awarded it. Strong performance in some areas cannot offset a clear gap elsewhere within that same area.

Inclusion is now a standalone graded area. Previously, how a school supported pupils with SEND was assessed indirectly, spread across other judgements. It is now inspected directly and visibly, in its own right.

The secure fit model, in concrete terms

Under the old single grade, a school with brilliant teaching and a patchy approach to SEND could still come away as "Good" overall, with the SEND gap absorbed into a broader positive picture. Under the new model, that same gap now shows up directly and visibly in the inclusion evaluation area, regardless of how strong everything else is. The practical effect for you is that inclusion can no longer hide behind strength in other areas.

What inspectors are actually looking for

Image placeholderHyper-realistic image reflecting evidence-led inspection practice · 16:9

Inspectors assess how well leaders have embedded inclusive practice, not simply whether the right policy documents exist. This is exactly why the work from the first two courses in this toolkit matters here: a published Inclusion Strategy with genuine evidence behind it, and a set of ISPs that reflect real, checkable practice, are the kind of evidence that stands up to this kind of scrutiny. A folder of unread policies does not.

Giving governors questions, not just answers

A briefing that only tells governors what your school is doing leaves them with nothing to test for themselves at the next meeting. Give them a small set of questions they can genuinely ask you. Click each card to see why it has more teeth than the version most schools settle for.

What to tell them: three questions

Structure your briefing around three questions:

1
Icon image

Where are we now?

A short summary of your Inclusion Strategy status, your progress on ISP-ready practice, and how your school accesses the Experts at Hand offer.

2
Icon image

What is changing, and when?

A clear distinction between what is already in force, such as Ofsted's inclusion judgement, and what is still proposed, such as the ISP statutory duty and the Inclusion Strategy's move toward a full legal footing.

3
Icon image

What do we need from you?

Specific asks, such as agreeing time for staff training, scrutinising evidence rather than policy documents at the next governor meeting, or signing off a budget line linked to your Inclusion Strategy activity.

Scrutinising the budget

Image placeholderHyper-realistic image reflecting governors scrutinising SEND spending · 16:9

Your Inclusion Strategy, covered in Course 1, sets out funded activity tied to your Inclusive Mainstream Fund allocation and your core budget. Governors are expected to scrutinise that spending, not just approve it once and move on, so give them a genuine opportunity to ask why a particular activity was chosen and how you will know if it worked, rather than presenting the budget as already decided.

Confirmed versus proposed

Avoid presenting this as a finished, settled picture. Being precise about what is confirmed and what is still developing is itself a mark of a leadership team that has genuinely understood the landscape, not a weakness in the briefing.

Image placeholderHyper-realistic image reflecting confirmed SEND reforms already in force · 16:9

Confirmed and already in force:

Ofsted's inclusion judgement, and the Experts at Hand offer's funding and delivery.

Image placeholderHyper-realistic image reflecting proposed SEND reforms and developing requirements · 16:9

Proposed and developing:

the Inclusion Strategy's move to a full statutory duty, and the ISP statutory requirement itself.

Keeping these two lists visibly separate in your briefing stops governors, or staff, from treating an emerging proposal as if it already carries the force of law.

Worked example: a governors’ meeting

Picture a SENCo using this briefing at a termly governors' meeting. Rather than presenting a lengthy update covering every change in SEND policy this year, she uses the three-question structure to focus the conversation: where the school stands today, what is confirmed versus still proposed, and one specific ask, agreement to fund a staff training afternoon on ISP-ready outcome writing. The meeting ends with a clear decision recorded in the minutes, rather than a general sense that "SEND is being looked at."

Worked example: an SLT briefing

The same structure looks different in an SLT context. A deputy head using this briefing at a senior leadership meeting is less likely to need the "confirmed versus proposed" section explained from scratch, since SLT should already be closer to the detail than governors are. Their version spends more time on the practical "what do we need from you" section: which staff need training and when, how referral routes into Experts at Hand get communicated to new starters, and who owns the next round of ISP sampling from Course 2. Where the governor version ends in a decision recorded in minutes, the SLT version should end in a named action with a date attached, feeding directly into the action plan you will build in the next module.
